The average Indian carrier runs through six shared metro hub belts

This is the network a typical large Indian parcel carrier would run, built from where the eight biggest carriers publicly say their hubs are. Six metro belts host most of them: Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Kolkata, Chennai and Hyderabad. The trunk lanes follow the Golden Quadrilateral, its two diagonals and the busiest toll roads. The Northeast hangs off a single road through Siliguri.

Who moves India's parcels (FY26)

India moves about 7.4 billion parcels (range 6.1–8.9), or 20 million a day.

Delhivery: FY26 express parcels, including Ecom Express[33]. Blue Dart: FY26 domestic shipments; includes business documents and B2B parcels[36]. India Post: FY26 domestic parcels (74.9 million); letters excluded[38]. Valmo (Meesho): 695 million in Apr–Sep 2025, doubled. Only the orders Valmo's own partner network carries; Meesho orders handed to Delhivery, Shadowfax or Xpressbees are in their counts[32]. Shadowfax: 726 million FY26 orders less about 140 million same-city (hyperlocal) orders, which never reach a hub[34]. Xpressbees: About 1.2 million a day in early 2025 (press report), times 365. The company publishes no count[35]. DTDC: Company figure of about 170 million a year, undated[37]. Amazon, Ekart and smaller carriers: Not published. What is left of the market after the networks that publish a count; the check against the broker's shares is above[31][32].

Where the sellers are

Meesho's prospectus is the only public count of sellers by state. Four states hold 54% of its sellers: Uttar Pradesh 15.9%, Gujarat 15.7%, Delhi 13.8% and Rajasthan 8.9%[41]. Most Uttar Pradesh sellers sit in the NCR districts (Gautam Buddha Nagar, Ghaziabad, Meerut)[43], and Flipkart names Delhi and Surat as its two biggest sources of new sellers[42]. We spread the remaining sellers by state GDP. Seller pickups by hub, as a share of all seller pickups: Delhi NCR 26%, Surat 9%, Jaipur 9%, Ahmedabad 5%, Mumbai 4%, Bengaluru 3%.

Estimated parcels sorted at each hub city

The fifteen busiest hub cities, all networks together. Pickups: parcels that start in the hub's area, from sellers or warehouses. Transhipment: parcels from elsewhere, re-sorted here on the way to somewhere else. Deliveries: parcels for people in the area.

B2B goods by state (estimated)

Every consignment of goods worth more than ₹50,000 needs an e-way bill, so the GST network's count is the best public measure of B2B freight. In FY26 there were 524 million inter-state e-way bills, about 1.4 million consignments a day, and 1,023 million within states[58]. Most parcels are below the threshold, so these barely overlap with the parcel counts above. B2B sources are not where parcel sellers are: Haryana sends the most, while Uttar Pradesh and Delhi lead parcel sellers.

#Containers by port (rail vs road)

Containers are where exim meets the inland network. Gateway ports handled about 74 thousand containers (TEU) a day from March to June 2026[59]; Mundra and JNPA alone take 65%. As a check, JNPA's own FY26 total was 8.17 million[60], close to this rate. Vizhinjam (about 3.8 thousand a day) is left out because its boxes go ship to ship. Only three ports publish how many containers leave by rail; for them the table splits rail from road. Rail containers stay on rail.

The port volumes come from terminal counts; the rail shares are what each port or CONCOR reported, for the period shown[61][62][62]. Mundra's and Pipavav's are older than JNPA's.

Empty running. A truck counted at a plaza may be empty. Where goods flow mostly one way, trucks come back light: if a link carries 97% of its goods one way and trucks return the way they came, at least 48% of its passages run empty (Guwahati – Silchar). That is arithmetic on the estimated direction, not a measurement; nothing public measures empty trucks.

Not shown on the map: links with no NHAI toll plaza, such as the Mumbai–Pune expressway, which is run by the state. B2B is not yet part of the choke points below.

Load by month: B2B and parcels together

Parcels a day: estimated, the long-distance parcels routed over the leg on an average day, all networks, both directions (see "Choke points for parcels"). Heavy trucks a day: measured, the busiest plaza on the leg. The two are not added: a truck is a truck, a parcel is one item in it, and the plazas already count the trucks that carry parcels. Parcels by hub follow the same quarters: no public source gives a hub's volume by month.

Choke points for parcels

Every long-distance parcel flow in the volume model above is routed along the shortest road on the map. Each link is as wide as the share of parcel flow crossing it. Ringed hubs are choke points; squares are ports. Then each hub and link is closed in turn to see what is cut off and how far the rest must detour. B2B goods and port containers are not included, because no public source says which roads they use (see above). Confidence: Low to Medium.

Parcel flow: share of long-distance parcel flow that crosses the hub or link (modelled). Heavy trucks a day: measured at the busiest toll plaza on the link, a floor for its truck traffic; blank for hubs, and "no plaza" where none on the link's highway could be placed. Most exposed zone: share of that zone's long-distance parcel flow (in and out) that depends on it. A long way round means the next route on this schematic map is more than 300 km longer. The map leaves out smaller parallel roads, so this shows how much the network leans on a corridor, not the route trucks would actually take.

How this was built

We collected 49 publicly reported facilities (gateways, sort centres, fulfilment centres with sortation) for 7 of the eight largest carriers, from company filings, press releases and trade press. Each facility is assigned to the metro hub belt it sits in. Knight Frank's 2026 warehousing report supplies the named warehousing clusters and the stock of the top eight markets.

A city's score counts how many carriers have a facility there, plus its warehousing weight. The tier follows from the score. Links come from the Golden Quadrilateral and its diagonals, the ten highest-earning toll plazas, the Bharatmala economic corridors and the dedicated freight corridors. The company-level facility list is kept in the project files, not on this page.

Parcel volumes: each network's FY26 count comes from its results or press reports (table above). The market total is the average of three anchors: Delhivery's count over its market share, Meesho's count over its share, and Redseer's industry total. Pickups come from warehouses or from sellers. Amazon and Ekart ship about 70% from their own warehouses, Valmo nothing, the rest about half. Warehouse parcels: 80% leave the top 8 markets, split by warehousing stock, the rest split across secondary markets. Seller parcels follow the seller map above. Deliveries follow a blend of population and state GDP; Valmo leans more on population. Transhipment: every pickup-to-delivery flow is routed along the shortest road path on the map. A parcel that starts outside a national gateway is consolidated at the first gateway on its path. One bound for a city that is not a gateway is broken down at the last gateway on its path. The splits and percentages are our assumptions, listed in the project files for review.

Choke points: the parcel flows are routed along the shortest road (freight rail excluded), and each hub and link is closed in turn to measure what depends on it. Port containers are shown as measured totals only; nothing public says which roads they use.

B2B lanes: each state's e-way bills sent and received over the last twelve months are spread over its hubs (as for parcel deliveries). A gravity model splits them between partner states: every state keeps its measured totals, and trade falls with road distance. Trips inside one hub belt count as 50 km (our assumption). Within-state bills are split the same way between the state's own hubs. Flows follow the shortest road. The distance setting and the measure (count or value) are the ones whose routed flows best match measured heavy trucks at the busiest plaza on each link, after one scale factor that absorbs everything a bill does not say: trucks per consignment, empty trucks, through traffic and goods that go by rail.

What this cannot tell you

  • No dated public source names facility locations for Xpressbees, so they add nothing to the scores.
  • India Post reports 188 parcel hubs but publishes no city list we could verify. Only its Thane sorting hub is counted.
  • Coverage is uneven. Amazon and Flipkart announce openings; others rarely do. Cities such as Indore and Vijayawada are probably under-scored.
  • Some sources are old (Blue Dart's Bhiwandi superhub dates from 2003). A facility may have moved or closed.
  • B2B lanes are estimated. Plaza counts check how much moves on a link, not which states it comes from or which way it goes. E-way bills also cover goods sent by rail, and one bill can be a carton or a full truck. Short trips near the plazas add trucks the model cannot see.
  • Hub volumes are modelled, not measured. Seller locations rest on one marketplace (Meesho); Amazon and Flipkart sellers may sit elsewhere. Mumbai's warehouse pickups are probably too high, because its warehouse stock includes much that is not parcels.
  • Transhipment assumes road routing through national gateways only. Direct trucks between big seller towns and distant gateways, air freight, and carriers' own regional hubs (for example at Nagpur or Lucknow) would move some of it elsewhere.
  • Blue Dart, DTDC and India Post carry business parcels as well as e-commerce, so they sit slightly outside the e-commerce market total. That makes the Amazon and Ekart remainder slightly low.
